                      experiment∗

                                          Abstract
           Algorithmic decision aids increasingly act on users’ behalf, and in many
       instances users can either retain control or delegate decisions to an algorithm
       altogether. A common design assumption is that personalizing an algorithm
       to users’ own preferences increases acceptance. Drawing on accounts of the
       intrinsic value of decision rights, we argue the opposite can hold: a rule that
       claims to decide as the user would substitutes for the user’s own judgment,
       making the loss of choice authority salient at the moment of delegation.
       In a preregistered between-subjects experiment (N = 233), participants
       repeatedly chose whether to decide themselves or to delegate to an algorithm
       that selected the option with the highest expected value (in one treatment)
       or implemented participants’ previously elicited preferences (in the other).
       Personalization did not increase delegation at any point of the task; instead,
       it deterred delegation in two ways. At first contact, participants were
       significantly less likely to delegate to the preference-based than to the
       optimization-based algorithm; in subsequent decisions, delegation rates
       were statistically indistinguishable. And second, categorical non-delegation
       (never delegating) was substantially more prevalent under personalization
       (15.7% vs. 3.4%, exploratory analysis). This resistance does not appear to be
       accuracy-based: participants expected the personalized algorithm to match
       their preferences better than the objective rule, yet were no more willing
       to delegate to it, and never-delegators emphasized retaining responsibility
       for the choice. Accuracy-based trust and authority-based resistance thus
       emerge as distinct channels of algorithm acceptance: personalization can
       improve the former while aggravating the latter.

1    Introduction
Algorithmic systems increasingly make and implement decisions on users’ be-
half in both everyday and high-stakes contexts, from automated driving to au-
tonomous trading and smart-home control. Public perceptions of algorithmic
decision-making vary substantially across domains and are shaped by perceived
fairness, accountability, privacy, trust, and usefulness (Aysolmaz et al., 2023). In
many such settings, users face a concrete choice: they can retain control and
decide themselves, or delegate the decision to an algorithm. Delegation can re-
duce cognitive effort and simplify complex choice tasks, but it can also reshape
perceived agency and responsibility for outcomes. Recent literature shows that
people do delegate consequential choices to AI systems, and that this delegation
depends on the decision context (Candrian & Scherer, 2022). Note that throughout
the paper, we refer to these systems interchangeably as algorithms, algorithmic
advice, and algorithmic decision aids. This reflects the practical reality that, from
a user’s perspective, the boundary between AI and algorithmic decision rules is
often opaque, and particularly so when we think about personalized systems.
    The idea of personalizing algorithmic systems is to tailor content, products,
and services to individual user preferences, which in turn should increase ac-
ceptance. Specifically, if individuals differ in preferences, then a decision aid
that incorporates those preferences should, in principle, deliver higher subjective
utility than a one-size-fits-all rule. In financial decision support, for example,
systems often elicit risk preferences and translate them into portfolio recommen-
dations. The key empirical question, however, is not whether personalization
sounds attractive in principle, but whether it actually increases willingness to
delegate.
    Existing evidence leaves that question open. In robo-advisor research, much
of the evidence focuses on what predicts whether people adopt robo-advisors
and how they use them (for example, Mahmud et al., 2025), rather than on direct
contrasts between preference-based personalization and objective delegation
rules. Adjacent work has shown that conversational onboarding and autonomy-
preserving designs shape trust and uptake (Fink et al., 2024; Hildebrand & Bergner,
2021). However, these studies vary the interface, degree of user control, or
communication format, not the nature of the underlying decision rule itself. To our
knowledge, no study has experimentally tested whether making an algorithm’s
decision rule preference-based, rather than objectively optimizing, changes actual

                                         2
delegation behavior. More generally, behavioral evidence shows that delegation
to algorithms is shaped by more than expected performance: people sometimes
avoid algorithms and sometimes prefer them based on the context (Dietvorst et
al., 2018; Logg et al., 2019); they need to understand how recommendations are
generated before accepting them (Yeomans et al., 2019); and acceptance depends
on moderators such as perceived control and task features (Chugunova & Sele,
2022; Mahmud et al., 2022).
     Preference-based personalization can therefore be double-edged. While it may
increase perceived fit, it may also encroach on users’ sense of choice authority:
a system that claims to decide as you would substitutes for personal judgment
more directly than an impersonal optimization rule, making delegation feel like
ceding active control rather than outsourcing a computation. Our own post-task
evidence is consistent with this interpretation: many categorical non-delegators
explicitly report wanting to keep responsibility for the choice, suggesting that
the barrier is not fear of being held responsible for a bad choice or outcome, but
rather a positive valuation of exercising choice authority. This reading aligns with
evidence that perceived control over one’s choices is a key driver of algorithm
acceptance (Schaap et al., 2024) in addition to expected benefits. It also connects
to findings that algorithmic transparency can paradoxically heighten resistance:
disclosing how a system decides can trigger negative social interpretations (Chen
et al., 2024) and induce pushback in algorithmic management contexts (Hu et al.,
2024), suggesting that transparency about a system’s decision basis may itself
contribute to a heightened sense of reduced agency.
     These issues are especially salient at first contact, when users have no experi-
ential basis for evaluating an algorithm and must judge based on abstract cues
about its decision logic. Stable preferences for decision autonomy can constrain
delegation even when delegation is instrumentally attractive (Ivanova-Stenzel &
Tolksdorf, 2025), making the perceived loss of choice authority especially salient
at the moment of delegation. Task characteristics matter too: trust and reliance
depend on perceived objectivity and time pressure (Yang et al., 2025). Skepti-
cism does not only operate before first contact, however: once delegation has
begun, reactions to algorithmic errors are an important driver of whether users
keep relying on a system. A robust finding is that people abandon algorithms
more readily than they switch away from doing tasks themselves or delegating
to another human after seeing comparable mistakes: algorithmic errors are, in
effect, forgiven less (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Renier et al., 2021). We will see that
this asymmetry shapes how participants respond to feedback in our setting as
well. Together, these factors suggest substantial skepticism about delegating to
algorithms, both at the point of adoption and in the face of subsequent errors.
     To understand what shapes delegation at first contact, we must study observ-
able behavior rather than attitudes alone. Behavioral reliance and self-reported

                                         3
trust need not coincide; evidence from AI-advisor interactions shows that deter-
minants of behavioral trust can diverge from self-reported trust (Schreibelmayr &
Mara, 2025). Moreover, even in repeated interaction settings, the mere framing of
advice as AI-generated can produce systematic overreliance (Klingbeil et al., 2024),
underscoring that label-driven cues persist beyond first contact. This motivates
our focus on observable delegation behavior, treating first contact as a critical
moment when abstract cues about an algorithm’s decision logic are especially
influential in shaping whether users delegate.
     In this study, we contrast two decision rules that are both plausible in fi-
nancial choice settings but may elicit different reactions. One algorithm follows
an objective optimization rule and selects the option with the highest expected
value (EV), which may appear impersonal and easier to justify as a rule-based
benchmark. The other is preference-based personalization in a narrow sense: it
predicts a participant’s preferred choice from their previously elicited preferences
rather than maximizing EV. In implementation terms, the personalized algorithm
maps each participant’s earlier choices in a simpler elicitation environment to
corresponding choices in a more complex environment later in the experiment; it
does not involve adaptive updating or learning during the delegation task.
     To test whether these two rules elicit different willingness to delegate, we run
a preregistered between-subjects experiment with repeated delegation decisions.
Participants first complete 12 binary lottery choices (Part 1), which provide the
preference-elicitation input for the personalized algorithm. They then enter Part 2,
where in each round they choose either to decide themselves among eight lottery
options or to delegate that choice to the assigned algorithm (EV or preference-
based, depending on the treatment).
     This design allows us to isolate a preregistered first-contact test, before any
outcome feedback is observed, and to distinguish it from behavior after feedback
has accumulated. The comparison between our algorithms connects to evidence
that responsibility-sharing considerations can shape advisor choice in human-
versus-algorithm settings (Gazit et al., 2023) and to recent studies of financial
delegation and outcome evaluation (Ismagilova & Ploner, 2025). Related findings
in financial delegation are also suggestive of responsibility-sharing considerations
(Werner et al., 2026), even though evidence on perceived responsibility differences
between human and machine partners is not uniform (Gazit et al., 2023; Kirchkamp
& Strobel, 2019).

Theoretical framework: delegation as ceding choice authority
Why should the content of an algorithm’s decision rule change willingness to del-
egate, holding constant that delegation is available, voluntary, and instrumentally
attractive? Our answer starts from the observation that choosing is not only a

                                         4
means to outcomes but carries value of its own. People derive procedural utility
from being the agent who decides (Frey et al., 2004), hold an intrinsic valuation of
decision rights that exceeds their instrumental value (Bartling et al., 2014), and pay
a measurable premium to retain control over payoff-relevant choices even when
ceding control would yield better expected outcomes (Owens et al., 2014). The
desire for control appears to be a basic psychological need with deep biological
roots (Leotti et al., 2010), and its frustration can trigger reactance, a motivational
state aimed at restoring threatened freedom of choice (Brehm, 1966). On this
view, delegation to an algorithm is a transfer-of-control decision (Candrian &
Scherer, 2022) whose psychological cost is not exhausted by expected outcomes:
it includes the forgone value of exercising choice authority.
     The decision rule matters because it shapes how this transfer of control is
construed. Delegating to an impersonal optimization rule can be framed as out-
sourcing a computation: the algorithm performs an objective calculation the user
could not (or would not) perform, and the user’s judgment remains conceptually
intact, much like using a calculator does not threaten one’s sense of being the
decision-maker. Delegating to a preference-based rule, by contrast, is harder to
construe this way. A system that claims to decide as the user would does not
complement the user’s judgment; it replaces it. If the algorithm already chooses
what the user would choose, the act of choosing itself becomes redundant, and
it is precisely this act that carries intrinsic value (Bartling et al., 2014; Owens
et al., 2014). Personalization thus makes the loss of choice authority salient in
a way that objective optimization does not, threatening autonomy as a basic
need (Deci & Ryan, 2000) and potentially evoking reactance (Brehm, 1966). This
construal-based account is consistent with evidence that perceived control, rather
than the algorithmic label per se, drives acceptance (Schaap et al., 2024), and
that stable preferences for decision autonomy constrain delegation even when
delegation pays (Ivanova-Stenzel & Tolksdorf, 2025).
     A lottery-choice paradigm suits this construal mechanism because it lets us
equate the two algorithms on everything except the feature our theory targets.
Payoffs and probabilities are stated unambiguously, so participants can judge
for themselves how each rule would choose and need no domain knowledge
to do so. Preferences are directly elicitable in Part 1 and thus known to the
experimenter, and expected value is computable from the displayed payoff table,
so the two rules can be equally simple to state and equally legitimate as decision
procedures, differing mainly in whether the optimized criterion is impersonal
or tied to the user’s own profile. Real financial or medical decisions could not
deliver this contrast cleanly: outcomes are ambiguous or deferred, preferences
must be inferred from noisy self-report or past behavior, and the “correct” choice
is unknown or contested. Thus, reluctance to delegate could reflect distrust of the
algorithm’s competence rather than a reaction to which criterion, the user’s own

                                          5
or an impersonal one, is being applied.
    This framework yields three testable implications. First, at first contact (when
no outcome feedback is available and construal of the decision rule is the domi-
nant cue) delegation should be lower to a preference-based than to an objective
algorithm, despite the former’s normative appeal (our preregistered H1 below).
Second, because the intrinsic valuation of decision rights is heterogeneous across
individuals (Bartling et al., 2014; Owens et al., 2014), resistance should be concen-
trated in a subgroup: users who place high value on choice authority should opt
out categorically, so categorical non-delegation should be more prevalent under
personalization (a supplementary, non-preregistered implication we examine
below). Third, the framework carries a mechanism signature that distinguishes it
from accuracy-based accounts: resistance to the personalized algorithm need not
be accompanied by lower expectations about its preference-matching accuracy,
whereas an accuracy-based account would require them. If users expect the
personalized rule to match their preferences at least as well as (or better than)
the objective rule, and yet delegate less, accuracy-based trust accounts become
hard to sustain and authority-based accounts become the more plausible reading,
although not the only possible one (see Section 5.4 on Limitations). Our post-task
expectation measures let us examine this implication, with the caveat that they
were collected after the incentivized rounds and ask how well the algorithm’s
choices would fit participants’ preferences, but not how much money they would
produce.

Preregistered hypothesis and analysis plan
Consistent with our preregistration (https://aspredicted.org/7un5qf.pdf), our
main hypothesis is that the diminished sense of choice authority from delegating
to a personalized algorithm outweighs the benefits it renders in terms of better-
fitting choices:3

H 1. People are more likely to delegate financial decisions to an EV-maximizing
algorithm than to a personalized algorithm that incorporates their (revealed)
preferences.

    Our preregistered primary analyses test this hypothesis in two complementary
ways. First, we test the treatment difference in delegation in the first decision
(round 1) using a one-sided Boschloo exact test. This isolates first contact, when
participants have no outcome feedback and must decide whether to delegate based
on their interpretation of the algorithm. Second, we test whether EV maximization

leads to higher delegation overall by comparing participants’ average delegation
frequencies across rounds using a one-sided Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test.
    To account for the repeated-decision structure and additional determinants of
delegation, our preregistration also specifies a multivariate regression analysis.
We estimate a model with treatment indicators interacted with the decision
period, decision-ID fixed effects, and participant-clustered standard errors. We
further include the feedback history in the form of including the share of zero
outcomes among prior delegated choices, to control for how experienced payoffs
may influence subsequent delegation decisions. This preregistered specification
tests whether any treatment difference varies systematically over decisions while
controlling for option-set heterogeneity and participants’ feedback histories.
Finally, the preregistration contains one secondary hypothesis on feedback: after
delegating to the algorithm and obtaining a payoff of zero, participants in the
preference-based condition are more likely to switch to non-delegation in the
following round than under any other circumstances, in particular than under
the same circumstances in the EV condition. We report this test in Section 4.5.
    Beyond our confirmatory test of H1, we exploratorily examine whether resis-
tance to personalization is concentrated in a subgroup (categorical non-delegation,
that is, never delegating across rounds, which captures a distinct form of opting
out of decision aids altogether) and whether authority-based and accuracy-based
accounts can be dissociated using post-task expectation data.
    In sum, this paper provides a preregistered test of a simple but consequential
claim: even in a setting where preference-based personalization may appear nor-
matively attractive, people may nonetheless be more willing to delegate to a ‘more
objective’ algorithm. The contribution is threefold: we provide a preregistered
behavioral comparison of objective versus personalized decision rules and show
that the treatment effect is concentrated at first contact; we document categorical
non-delegation as a distinct uptake barrier under personalization; and we show
that this resistance arises despite participants crediting the personalized algorithm
with superior preference-matching, which is more consistent with an authority-
based than with an accuracy-based barrier to algorithm adoption. Together, these
findings inform both the theoretical understanding of algorithm aversion as a
phenomenon with distinct accuracy-based and autonomy-based channels, and
the design implication that uptake depends on perceived rule characteristics, not
only on expected performance.


2    Related Work
Our work sits at the intersection of literatures on (i) the behavioral uptake of
algorithmic decision aids, (ii) delegation, agency, and responsibility in human–


                                         7
algorithm interaction, (iii) transparency as an interpretive cue for acceptance,
(iv) personalization as a design feature of algorithmic decision rules, and (v)
heterogeneity in trust and delegation. Below, we situate our study within these
streams, identify the specific gap our experiment addresses—the lack of a direct
behavioral comparison between personalized and objective decision rules—and
connect them to the robo-advisor application domain.

2.1    Algorithm aversion, appreciation, and conditions for
       uptake
Behavioral uptake of algorithmic advice is not one-directional: studies document
both algorithm aversion and algorithm appreciation, depending on context, feed-
back, and comparison benchmark (Dietvorst et al., 2018; Logg et al., 2019). A core
implication is that reliance cannot be predicted based on objective performance
alone. Users need to make sense of how recommendations are generated before
accepting them, and understandability can affect uptake independently of accu-
racy (Yeomans et al., 2019). Systematic evidence further shows that uptake is
moderated by factors at multiple levels (algorithm, individual, and task), including
perceived control and decision context (Chugunova & Sele, 2022; Mahmud et al.,
2022).
    This mixed-evidence baseline motivates our design choice to study delega-
tion as an observed behavioral outcome and to isolate one concrete determi-
nant of uptake: the algorithmic decision rule (objective EV maximization versus
preference-based personalization).

2.2    Delegation, agency, and responsibility sharing
Delegation goes beyond advice-taking: it is a transfer-of-control decision that
entails ceding choice authority to the algorithm (Candrian & Scherer, 2022). For
that reason, agency and responsibility become central concerns. In advisor-choice
settings, people evaluate more than expected payoff, including whether responsi-
bility for consequences can be shared with or offloaded onto the advisor (Gazit
et al., 2023). Related evidence from financial delegation suggests that delegation
patterns are consistent with responsibility-sharing motivations, particularly in
loss contexts (Werner et al., 2026). Yet, evidence on whether perceived respon-
sibility differs between human and machine partners is not uniform: Gazit et
al. (2023) build on studies reporting such differences, whereas Kirchkamp and
Strobel (2019) find little difference in perceived responsibility or guilt.
     Beyond responsibility considerations, a well-established body of evidence
shows that people attach value to holding decision rights as such. Bartling
et al. (2014) demonstrate that decision rights carry intrinsic value beyond their

                                         8
instrumental benefits; Owens et al. (2014) document a “control premium” whereby
individuals sacrifice expected payoff to retain authority over choices; and Frey et
al. (2004) argue that the process of deciding generates utility independently of
outcomes. In the delegation-to-algorithms context specifically, Ivanova-Stenzel
and Tolksdorf (2025) show that stable autonomy preferences constrain delegation
even when it is instrumentally attractive. Our theoretical framework builds
directly on this evidence: if choice authority is intrinsically valued, decision rules
that make its loss more salient should face stronger resistance.

2.3    Transparency, and interpretive cues for acceptance
Personalization and transparency are often presented as acceptance-enhancing
design features, but recent evidence suggests they can also trigger resistance. In
recommendation contexts, greater transparency can backfire when users infer
socially negative meanings (for example, feeling judged or dehumanized), reduc-
ing acceptance (Chen et al., 2024). In algorithmic management, transparency can
likewise show a non-monotonic effect, where additional transparency eventually
increases resistance rather than compliance (Hu et al., 2024). Related evidence
further indicates that acceptance is driven less by whether the decision-maker
is labeled algorithmic or human, and more by expected benefits and perceived
control (Schaap et al., 2024).
     The broader explainability literature is consistent with this interpretation:
transparency and explanations matter, but their effects depend on what they
change psychologically. Fairness/accountability/transparency perceptions shape
trust and adoption (Shin & Park, 2019), as do perceived trustworthiness dimensions
of algorithmic decision-makers (Höddinghaus et al., 2021); explanations can
improve trust calibration and behavior in some settings (Leichtmann et al., 2023);
and combining explanation with decision control can improve compliance, though
design complexity can also offset those gains (Westphal et al., 2023). In applied
decision-support experiments, transparency influences advice use via trusting
beliefs and perceived understanding, with effects that depend on the information
environment and experienced system performance (Ning et al., 2024; Sun et al.,
2026). For our setting, this implies that users’ interpretation of decision logic can
shape delegation independently of objective expected returns.

2.4    Decision-rule comparison gap: personalized versus ob-
       jective rules
Despite the breadth of the literatures reviewed above, a striking gap remains:
to our knowledge, no study has tested whether making an algorithm’s decision
rule preference-based (as opposed to following an impersonal, objective criterion)

                                          9
changes actual delegation behavior. Several lines of work come close but address
different questions.
    First, the “algorithm modification” paradigm pioneered by Dietvorst et al.
(2018) shows that giving users even slight control over an algorithm’s output
increases adoption, although preserving human involvement can come at the cost
of lower accuracy (Sele & Chugunova, 2024). This demonstrates that user input
matters, but the manipulation concerns users’ ability to adjust the output, not
whether the underlying decision rule incorporates their preferences or maximizes
an objective criterion (in fact, in our study participants prefer the algorithm where
‘their input matters’ less, not more).
    Second, the robo-advisor design literature examines how features such as
conversational onboarding (Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021), user controllability
over rebalancing (S. Kim et al., 2025), and the performance–control trade-off in
configuration choices (Rühr, 2020) shape adoption and trust. These studies vary
the interface and the degree of control, but hold the nature of the decision rule
itself constant or leave it as an implicit design feature.
    Third, theoretical and empirical work on robo-advisor personalization has fo-
cused on optimizing portfolio allocation through more granular client interaction
(Capponi et al., 2022) or on comparing algorithm-selected portfolios with self-
directed choices (Boulu-Reshef et al., 2026). These contributions are informative
about performance implications of personalization, but do not ask whether users
are more or less willing to delegate when the algorithm’s rule is personalized.
    This gap is consequential beyond academic interest. In practice, financial
robo-advisors, healthcare decision aids, and algorithmic recommender systems
routinely face the design choice between objective optimization criteria (such as
expected-value or evidence-based guidelines) and preference-based rules that in-
corporate individual user profiles. If personalization reduces rather than increases
the willingness to delegate, as our evidence suggests, then system designers may
inadvertently raise adoption barriers by prioritizing preference-matching. Un-
derstanding this trade-off is directly relevant for any domain where algorithmic
decision aids must be voluntarily adopted to be effective.

2.5    Heterogeneity and contextual moderators of trust and
       delegation
Recent evidence shows that algorithm uptake is heterogeneous across people,
tasks, and decision environments (Ferraz et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). At the
individual level, delegation varies with psychological readiness and other personal
traits linked to delegation behavior (Ferraz et al., 2025; H. Kim, 2026). Beyond
stable traits, uptake also depends on decision architecture: autonomy-preserving


                                         10
choice designs can increase recommendation acceptance (Fink et al., 2024). At
the task level, uptake depends on objectivity and time pressure (Yang et al., 2025),
while users sometimes follow advice that contradicts their own information, a
form of overreliance (Klingbeil et al., 2024). Taken together, these cross-level
moderators imply that average delegation rates can mask meaningful subgroup
patterns. For our study, this motivates looking beyond means and explicitly report-
ing categorical non-delegation as a distinct acceptance pattern. The documented
heterogeneity in the intrinsic valuation of decision rights (Bartling et al., 2014;
Owens et al., 2014) provides a theoretical reason to expect exactly such a subgroup
structure: if a subset of users values choice authority strongly, personalization-
induced resistance could manifest as categorical opt-out in that subgroup rather
than as a uniform shift in delegation rates.

2.6    Financial advice and robo-advisors as an application
       domain
Although our task is experimentally stylized, it maps naturally to financial del-
egation settings where users decide whether to retain control or hand choice
authority to an algorithmic decision aid. This is exactly the context in which objec-
tive optimization and preference-based personalization often coexist in practice.
Existing robo-advisor evidence is informative but fragmented: studies document
adoption patterns and how users combine advisor recommendations with their
own judgment (Figà-Talamanca et al., 2022; Mahmud et al., 2025), outcome bias
in the evaluation of delegated financial decisions, whether made by a human or
an algorithm (Ismagilova & Ploner, 2025), and potential performance gains from
robo-advice relative to self-directed choices (Lambrecht et al., 2026). Because both
kinds of rules coexist in robo-advice, financial decision-making is a strong test
bed for our question: whether changing the algorithmic decision rule changes
willingness to delegate, even when both rules are plausible.

2.7    Synthesis and research gap
Across these literatures, the common message is that delegation to algorithms is
jointly shaped by decision-rule design, perceived control and responsibility, trust
calibration, and context-dependent interpretation of transparency and autonomy-
related design cues (Chugunova & Sele, 2022; Dietvorst et al., 2018; Fink et al.,
2024; Hu et al., 2024; Schaap et al., 2024). Yet as we have documented above, the
existing evidence base contains a notable gap: while adjacent work has studied
algorithm modification, controllability, conversational design, and performance
implications of personalized portfolios, no study has experimentally isolated the


                                         11
effect of the decision rule itself, specifically, whether making the rule preference-
based rather than objectively optimizing changes users’ willingness to delegate.
This gap matters practically because robo-advisors, healthcare decision aids, and
algorithmic recommender systems routinely face exactly this design choice, and a
wrong assumption about the direction of the effect could raise rather than lower
adoption barriers.
     Our study addresses this gap with a preregistered behavioral test in a repeated-
decision delegation paradigm, contrasting an EV-maximizing rule with a preference-
based rule. It connects classic aversion/ appreciation evidence with newer findings
on autonomy, transparency backfire, and responsibility attribution (Fink et al.,
2024; Gazit et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Logg et al., 2019), and anchors the pre-
dicted direction of the effect in the economics and psychology of choice authority
(Bartling et al., 2014; Brehm, 1966; Deci & Ryan, 2000; Frey et al., 2004; Leotti et
al., 2010; Owens et al., 2014). More broadly, our focus on the decision rule as a
determinant of delegation speaks to recent calls to move the study of human–AI
interaction in finance from model performance toward decision architecture, and
in particular, toward how authority, oversight, and accountability are allocated
between human and algorithm (Kou et al., 2026); our results suggest that where
this authority is perceived to reside can itself be shaped by how personalized the
decision rule appears.


3     Method
3.1    Participants and preregistered exclusions
Participants were recruited via Prolific from a UK-based participant pool and
completed the study online. The preregistered target was 120 participants per
treatment condition before exclusions. 240 participants completed the study.
After applying our preregistered exclusion rules for data quality and protocol
compliance (failed bot/slider checks at first attempt, failed comprehension checks
after the second attempt, failure to confirm careful participation, disclosed AI
usage during the study, and implausibly short completion times under the pre-
registered log-time rule), the final analysis sample is N = 233 (EV condition: n =
118; preference-based condition: n = 115). Of the 7 excluded participants, 1 failed
the bot/slider check, 4 failed the careful-participation check (one of whom also
disclosed AI usage), and 3 disclosed AI usage during the study (one overlapping
with the careful-participation failure); no participant was excluded for failing a
comprehension check or under the log-completion-time rule.




                                        12
3.2    Design and task
The study used a between-subjects design with two algorithm conditions. In both
conditions, participants completed a two-part experiment programmed in the
online-ready version of z-Tree (version 6.0.0 beta 69, Fischbacher, 2007).
     Part 1 consisted of 12 binary lottery choices used to elicit revealed risk prefer-
ences. Every lottery in the experiment specified a payoff for each of ten equally
likely states of the world, so that the expected value of an option is the average
of its ten payoffs (see Appendix A for all payoff matrices). Eight of these tasks
were directly linked to Part 2 (see below); the remaining four served as filler
tasks, as one of several measures to make it harder for participants to infer, while
working through Part 2, how its tasks correspond to those of Part 1. Participants
were truthfully told that “[t]he questions in this part are hypothetical, but the
choices may be meaningful for Part 2 (recall that your bonus payment from
the experiment depends on what happens in Part 2).” The highlighted words were
boldfaced in the original instructions.
     We deliberately designed Part 1 to give participants every opportunity to
make a well-considered, informed choice, precisely because these are the choices
on which the preference-based algorithm is based. To this end, three features
distinguish Part 1 from the later delegation task. First, unlike the time-constrained
decisions in Part 2 (see below), participants faced no time limit in Part 1 and could
deliberate for as long as they wished. Second, each Part-1 task presented a clean
choice between only two lotteries, rather than the eight options participants
would later face in Part 2. Third, payoffs were matched to states so that they
would appear in decreasing order to ease comparison, as can be seen in the upper
panel of Figure 1. In addition, we asked participants after each task in Part 1 how
confident they were in their choice.
     In Part 2, participants completed eight incentivized decisions between eight
lottery options each, as exemplified in the lower panel of Figure 1. These eight
Part-2 tasks were derived from eight mapped Part-1 binary tasks (all but the four
filler tasks). Specifically, each mapped Part-1 task provided two “anchor” options;
for Part 2, we first transformed each anchor option by permuting payoffs across
states (Option 6 and Option 3 in the lower panel of Figure 1), and then added
three decoy options for each anchor option. Each added option was state-wise
dominated by one of the anchor options (i.e., worse in at least one outcome and
no better in any other), without making the dominance relationship immediately
obvious from the displayed table. This yields eight options per Part-2 task (two
transformed anchors plus six decoy options). The within-task display order
of the eight options was set using a fixed pre-specified rule rather than per-
task randomization. We did this because unconstrained randomization would
occasionally produce display orders where the three decoy options belonging


                                          13
Figure 1: Example screens from Part 1 (preference elicitation, upper panel) and
Part 2 (delegation decisions with lottery choices, lower panel).

to the same anchor would appear consecutively next to the anchor option itself.
This is something we wanted to avoid, as such a clustering could have alerted
attentive participants to the underlying construction logic of Part-2 tasks. The
fixed ordering rule was designed to interleave anchor-based groups so that no
such visually suspicious pattern would arise. Note also that the ordering rule was
different between option sets.
     The order of the 12 Part-1 tasks was randomized with one pre-defined con-
straint: positions 11 and 12 were occupied by two (randomly selected) tasks from
the four Part-1 filler tasks that had no mapped counterpart in Part 2. Consequently,
all eight mapped Part-1 tasks appeared within the first ten Part-1 positions, while
still preserving randomization of task order subject to this constraint.
     The procedure for each round of Part 2 was as follows: Participants saw
the full table for five seconds. Then, they saw a different screen on which they
had five seconds to choose between delegating to the algorithm corresponding
to their treatment condition and choosing an option themselves. In the EV
condition, delegation selected the lottery with the highest expected value. In the
preference-based condition, delegation selected the non-dominated lottery which
the participant had preferred in Part 1 over the other non-dominated lottery from
the current task. Importantly, in both treatments the delegated option was always
one of the two transformed anchor options and therefore never one of the six
decoy options. Assignment to condition was randomized at the participant level.
In the EV condition, participants were informed that “[the] algorithm works out
how much each option would yield on average, and selects the option for which


                                        14
this is highest.” In the preference-based condition, they instead read that “[the]
algorithm analyses your choices from Part 1 and selects the best option for you
in light of your Part-1 choices.”
     If they did not delegate, they saw the table containing the eight options for up
to eight more seconds, during which they had to choose one of the options. After
each Part-2 decision, feedback provided full resolution of uncertainty: participants
were shown the chosen option and the realized payoff from the randomly drawn
state of the world for that round, together with the full table of options and
payoffs. If they did not make a choice in the payoff-relevant task, they did not
earn a bonus payment from the experiment. The total payoff from the experiment
was GBP 2.00 plus the points earned in one randomly selected round of Part 2,
converted at a rate of 1 penny per point (participants could earn up to GBP 8.90 in
addition to the base payoff). On average, participants earned GBP 4.14 including
the base payoff for a median time of just under 16 minutes.

3.3     Procedure
After being welcomed to the study, participants saw a symbolic representation
of a bicycle (two red tires and two triangles in yellow and green that together
formed the frame), being asked to indicate what that figure represented (a banana,
a traffic light, darts games, a bicycle, a robot, or ‘nothing, it’s randomly placed
shapes’). At the time, the agentic AIs would typically choose the traffic light, pre-
sumably because of the colouring of the shapes making up the bicycle. Following
that, participants would read a broad overview of the experiment, including the
range of possible payments (GBP 2 for participating and GBP 0 to GBP 8.90 as a
bonus, depending on decisions and on chance). Then, they would have to give
consent to participating and to our using of their data for the purposes of this
publication, which was followed by a final attention and ‘system’ check (‘Please
select “Disagree” on the following question’ and ‘please click on the white line in
such a way that the appearing blue dot is close to a value of 50.’).4
    After these checks, Part 1 (preference elicitation) started, followed by a ques-
tion on how satisfied they were with their choices from Part 1 overall. Then,
participants completed Part 2 (delegation decisions with round-by-round out-
come feedback), followed by a question of whether they would delegate to the
algorithm in case they had to choose to delegate or not for 10 (hypothetical) addi-
tional tasks. After indicating that choice, participants estimated (on a 0–10 integer
scale) in how many of those 10 tasks the algorithm would choose a non-preferred
option and in how many it would choose a “completely weird” option. Before the

3.5    Preregistered outcomes and analyses

4      Results
4.1    Overview
Two empirical patterns organize the results. First, treatment differences are
concentrated at first contact. Second, categorical non-delegation is substantially
more common under preference-based personalization. Both patterns match the
implications derived from the choice-authority framework in Section 1, and (as
we show in Section 4.6) they arise despite participants crediting the personalized
algorithm with superior preference-matching, the mechanism signature that
separates authority-based from accuracy-based accounts.

4.2    Primary test: first-contact delegation
As a benchmark for interpreting delegation behavior, choice quality among non-
delegated decisions was low in both treatments. The share of non-dominated
self-chosen options was 34.1% in the EV condition and 35.1% in the preference-
based condition, implying that roughly two-thirds of self-made choices were
dominated. Hence, participants had a clear instrumental reason to delegate. On
average, delegated decisions yielded 243.49 points (GBP 2.43), compared to 195.18
points (GBP 1.95) for non-delegated decisions, a difference of approximately 25%.
    In round 1, delegation was higher in the EV condition than in the preference-
based condition (66.9% vs. 55.8%). The preregistered one-sided Boschloo exact
test yields p = 0.041, supporting H1 for first-contact behavior. The corresponding
effect size is a risk difference of 11.1 percentage points (Wald 95% CI [−1.3, 23.7])
and an odds ratio of 1.60 (Fisher’s exact two-sided 95% CI [0.91, 2.84]). Note that
the confidence intervals span 0 and 1, respectively, because we are reporting
two-sided intervals here, while the pre-registered tests were one-sided tests.
    Figure 2 visualizes the round-level profile and shows that there is a clear
treatment gap in the first decision.

4.3    Primary test: delegation across repeated decisions
At the participant level, the preregistered one-sided Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney
test on average delegation across Part 2 yields W = 6428 and p = 0.243. The

     Descriptively, this non-difference reflects post-first-contact convergence that
is driven by a strong decline in delegation in the EV condition after round 1
(Figure 2). We treat this pattern as descriptive because our design was not op-
timized to identify why delegation declines more under EV after first contact.
Notwithstanding, we will report on how this pattern relates to ‘wins’ (rounds
in which the realized outcome was positive) and ‘losses’ (rounds in which the
realized outcome was zero). We do not read the convergence as evidence that
authority-based resistance to personalization is overridden by performance feed-
back. Two exploratory observations help to read it. First, average delegation in
rounds 2–8 is the net of two offsetting differences of about six to seven percentage
points each: more participants opt out entirely under personalization (Section 4.4),
while the average delegation rate among participants who delegate at least once
is lower under EV (49.8% vs. 56.4%). Second, part of the decline under EV is linked
to the algorithm’s picks. An EV pick that differs from the participant’s own pre-
ferred option pays zero more often and is followed by less delegation (Section 4.5);
had the 28 round-1 EV delegators with such a pick continued as often as those
whose pick matched their preference, round-2 delegation under EV would have
been about six percentage points higher (participant-level bootstrap 95% CI [0.4,
11.8]). By contrast, on an aggregate level participants react to a zero payoff in

zero payoffs overall (40.3% vs. 36.2% of delegated decisions; participant-clustered
logit p = 0.178). Note that the decompositions are descriptive and imprecise, so
a substantial part of the larger decline under EV remains unexplained.

4.4    Categorical non-delegation
This section reports the results of an exploratory analysis of categorical non-
delegation that is complementary to the preregistered primary tests. The second
clear pattern to be found in the data is categorical non-delegation, which can
be seen from the left end of Figure 3. In the preference-based condition, 18 of
115 participants never delegated (15.7%); in the EV condition, this applies to only
4 of 118 participants (3.4%). A one-sided Boschloo exact test (in line with the
directional prediction of the choice-authority framework that there will be more
categorical non-delegation under personalization) yields p < 0.001 (two-sided:
p = 0.001). The corresponding effect size is a risk difference of 12.3 percentage
points (Wald 95% CI [4.9, 19.7]) and an odds ratio of 5.25 (95% CI [1.65, 22.07],
based on Fisher’s exact test). This gap points to a distinct acceptance barrier under
personalization for a subset of users.7 Note that although there also seems to be a
treatment difference in always-delegators, this difference is far less pronounced
(19.1% vs. 13.6%). The subgroup structure of the effect is consistent with the
heterogeneity implication of the choice-authority framework: if the intrinsic
valuation of decision rights is concentrated in a subset of users (Bartling et al.,
2014; Owens et al., 2014), personalization-induced resistance should manifest
primarily as categorical opt-out rather than as a uniform downward shift in
delegation.

4.5    Responses to feedback
We analyze how participants react to feedback in four steps that differ in status
and in what they add. First, the preregistered secondary hypothesis asks whether
a zero payoff from a delegated choice makes participants in the preference-based
condition more likely to abandon delegation than those in the EV condition (con-
firmatory). Second, a win-stay/lose-shift model asks whether the two conditions
react differently to any prior win or loss, using all rounds 2–8 rather than only
rounds that follow a delegation (exploratory). In all regressions of this subsection
the outcome is switching, so a positive coefficient means more switching; the
exploratory models count any change of the previous round’s delegation status
as a switch, whereas the preregistered test only counts switches from delegation
to non-delegation. Third, we ask whether a loss weighs more heavily when the
algorithm rather than the participant produced it (exploratory). Fourth, we ask
whether the EV algorithm’s picks that differ from the participant’s own preferred
option are followed by less delegation (exploratory). The take-home message is
that losses, and especially losses produced by the algorithm, push participants
away from delegation in both conditions, with no evidence that this reaction is
stronger under personalization; the EV algorithm’s picks that differ from one’s
own preference are an additional, exploratory candidate for why delegation
declines more under EV.

Preregistered test: switching to non-delegation after a zero payoff
In the preregistration, we suggested one secondary hypothesis on how partic-
ipants respond to feedback: after delegating to the algorithm and obtaining a
payoff of zero, participants in the preference-based condition are more likely to
switch to non-delegation in the following round than under any other circum-
stances, in particular than under the same circumstances in the EV condition
(“participants are less forgiving with respect to the PREF algorithm”). We did not
pre-specify a test for this hypothesis. Here, we analyse it in a linear probability
model of switching to non-delegation in those rounds 2–8 that follow a delegated
round (799 observations from 209 participants), with task and decision-number
fixed effects and participant-clustered standard errors. The regressors are an
indicator for the preference-based condition, an indicator for a zero payoff in the
previous (delegated) round, and their interaction; the preregistered prediction
corresponds to a positive interaction, which we test one-sided.
    The data do not support our hypothesis. Participants did switch to non-
delegation much more often after a zero payoff than after a positive payoff from
a delegated choice, in both conditions (EV: 55.6% vs. 30.3%; preference-based:
45.3% vs. 21.5%). In the regression, this zero-payoff effect amounts to 0.254 (SE =


                                        23
0.048) in the EV condition and 0.250 (SE = 0.048) in the preference-based condition
(p < 0.001 in both). It is, however, not larger in the preference-based condition:
the interaction is slightly negative and insignificant (β = −0.023, SE = 0.067,
one-sided p = 0.633; two-sided p = 0.734). The conclusion does not depend
on how “any other circumstances” is read. Restricting attention to rounds that
follow a zero-payoff delegation, switching to non-delegation was, if anything,
less frequent in the preference-based than in the EV condition (β = −0.105, SE
= 0.070, two-sided p = 0.140). Comparing instead with all other rounds 2–8,
including those that follow a self-chosen option or a positive payoff, yields an
insignificant interaction of the wrong sign (β = −0.071, SE = 0.071, one-sided
p = 0.840; 1,615 observations from 233 participants). Hence, participants were
not less forgiving with respect to the preference-based algorithm.

Exploratory: win-stay/lose-shift
The remaining analyses in this subsection were not preregistered and are ex-
ploratory. To check whether the conclusion above depends on the narrow prereg-
istered definition, we estimate a win-stay/lose-shift model on all rounds 2–8, with
the full specification and estimates in Appendix B. Participants in both treatments
are more likely to switch after a loss than after a win, and the size of this reaction
does not differ between treatments (treatment × prior win: β = −0.009, SE =
0.049, p = 0.857). The one treatment difference is a level shift: participants in the
EV condition switch more often after a loss (β = 0.105, SE = 0.050, p = 0.036) and
by a similar amount after a win. This reflects a general tendency of EV participants
to switch more often rather than a stronger reaction to losses.

Exploratory: who produced the loss?
A more revealing picture emerges when we distinguish who produced the prior
outcome, that is, whether the previous round was delegated (so that “the algo-
rithm lost or won”) or self-chosen. Estimating the win-stay/lose-shift model
separately by treatment and interacting a prior loss with prior delegation, the
prior-loss × prior-delegation interaction is positive and significant in both condi-
tions (EV: β = 0.185, SE = 0.070, p = 0.009; preference-based: β = 0.140, SE = 0.064,
p = 0.030). That is, a loss prompts more switching in general, but a loss that the
algorithm produced prompts substantially more switching than a self-inflicted
loss. This is visible in the raw switching rates: after a delegated loss, the switching
rate is the highest in both conditions (45.3% in the preference-based and 55.6% in
the EV condition), well above the switching rate after a delegated win (21.5% and
30.3%) and also above that after a self-chosen loss (32.3% and 41.7%). Note that
“switching” means moving to self-choice after a delegated outcome but moving


                                          24
to delegation after a self-chosen one, so these comparisons are descriptive. This
exploratory pattern connects to the broader finding that algorithmic mistakes
tend to be forgiven less readily than one’s own (Dietvorst et al., 2018; Renier
et al., 2021): participants abandon delegation disproportionately after the algo-
rithm delivers a bad outcome, even though delegated choices were on average
substantially more profitable.

Exploratory: when the algorithm’s pick differs from one’s own preference
Under the preference-based algorithm, the delegated option is by construction
the participant’s own preferred option. Under EV, it coincides with that option
in about two thirds of delegated rounds. Table 2 therefore splits the delegated
rounds 1–7 into preference-based delegations and EV delegations whose pick
matches or differs from the participant’s own preferred option. EV picks that
differ from the participant’s preference pay zero more often (50.8%, against 36.3%
for matching EV picks and 36.0% under preference-based delegation) and are
followed by less delegation, especially after a zero payoff. In linear probability
models with participant-clustered standard errors, delegation after matching EV
picks is 8.4 percentage points lower than under preference-based delegation (95%
CI [−18.7, 2.0], p = 0.112), an estimate that is compatible with no difference,
but also with a sizeable reduction. After mismatching EV picks it is 14.5 points
lower (95% CI [−26.9, −2.0], p = 0.023; 16.4 points, p = 0.012 with task fixed
effects); the two EV groups do not differ significantly from each other (6.1 points,
p = 0.266). Controlling for a zero payoff reduces the gap after mismatching picks
only partly (to 10.8 points, p = 0.078). Within the EV condition, a zero payoff
lowers continued delegation by 20.0 points after a matching and by 36.7 points
after a mismatching pick (difference: 16.7 points, p = 0.083). The mismatch
is, however, not randomly assigned, since it depends on the participant’s own
preferences, and the pooled EV gap falls only from 10.3 to 9.1 points when a
zero payoff is controlled for. We therefore read these results as pointing to one
contributing channel, not as a full account of why delegation declines more under
EV. The forgone payoffs that the feedback screen showed (the best available option
minus the realized payoff) predict continued delegation on their own but not once
a zero payoff is controlled for (unreported analysis, available in the supplementary
materials).

Did participants trust the personalized algorithm to match their prefer-
ences?
A natural concern about our design is that lower delegation under personalization
might simply reflect participants doubting that the preference-based algorithm
captured their preferences in the first place. The expectation data speak directly
against this concern. After the incentivized rounds, participants estimated, out
of 10 hypothetical further tasks, in how many tasks the algorithm would pick
a non-preferred option and in how many it would pick a “completely weird”
option. Participants in the preference-based condition expected the algorithm
to make markedly fewer non-preferred choices than those in the EV condition
(on average 3.78 vs. 5.00 out of 10; t = −4.15, p < 0.001; Wilcoxon rank-sum
p < 0.001). Expectations about “completely weird” choices were also lower but
not significantly so (1.57 vs. 1.78; t = −0.94, p = 0.351). Participants thus
clearly credited the personalized algorithm with doing its job: relative to objective
optimization, it was expected to select options aligned with their preferences
substantially more often, without any rise in the perceived risk of grossly erratic
choices. This holds even for the participants who opted out entirely: within the
preference-based condition, never-delegators expected just as few non-preferred
choices as delegators (3.83 vs. 3.77 out of 10; t = −0.11, p = 0.912). Categorical
non-delegation under personalization thus cannot be attributed to a belief that
the algorithm failed to capture one’s preferences, reinforcing the earlier finding
(Section 4.4) that these never-delegators were, if anything, slightly more satisfied
with the Part-1 choices that fed the algorithm. Restricting the sample to the 21%

of participants who expect at most two mistakes leaves the treatment differences
at least as large (for example, round-1 delegation of 100% under EV against 72.7%
under preference-based delegation), although these subgroups are small and
self-selected (see the replication code).

Which reasons provided are most tightly linked to delegation?
This makes the reduced delegation under personalization all the more striking.
Even though participants believed the preference-based algorithm would more
often choose in line with their preferences, they were no more (and at first
contact significantly less) willing to delegate to it. The barrier therefore does
not appear to lie in doubts about the algorithm’s accuracy. Consistent with this,
the two explanation-demand items (“I find it unpleasant that I don’t know how
exactly the computer chooses” and “I would have preferred more information
about how the computer makes its decisions”) were selected only infrequently,
as was the profiling-related item (“I don’t like to be analysed by an algorithm”;
see Figure 4 below). Participants’ reservations were thus not primarily about
understanding, transparency, or competence at preference-matching, pointing
instead toward concerns about ceding choice authority itself, as we will show
below. This is precisely the mechanism signature derived in Section 1: higher
perceived preference-fit combined with equal or lower delegation speaks against
accuracy-based trust accounts and is consistent with authority-based resistance
(although, as noted in Section 5.4, the mechanism is not causally identified).

What distinguishes delegators from never-delegators?
Figure 4 shows the closed-item reasons that participants provided for their choice
(not) to delegate the task for another hypothetical 10 rounds. Note that the
percentages discussed in this subsection pool delegators and never-delegators
across both treatments, whereas Figure 4 itself displays the same items split
by treatment condition; the two views are complementary but not numerically
identical, and the pooled figures reported here cannot be read directly off the by-
treatment bars. We nonetheless display them separately to show that the reasons
vary more by delegation type than by treatment. The only visible treatment
contrast (larger never-delegator/delegator gaps in the EV condition) we do not
interpret because of the low case numbers.
    In terms of the difference between delegators and never-delegators, two pat-
terns stand out. First, participants who delegated at least once were far more
likely to cite performance-based motives: 70.1% indicated that the computer would
choose better, against only 36.4% of never-delegators (that they would choose
better). Second, never-delegators more often selected reasons centered on general


                                        27
distrust of the algorithm (50.0% vs. 32.2%; recall, however, that those who indicate
the strongest belief in that the algorithm does its job do not exhibit a smaller
treatment effect; if at all, the effect is stronger in that subset) and on responsibility
for the choice (63.6% vs. 38.9%; see the note on this item below). At first glance,
the prevalence of the “distrust” item among never-delegators might seem to sit
uneasily with our claim that resistance is not accuracy-based: the expectation
data (above) show that participants, including never-delegators, expected the
personalized algorithm to match their preferences at least as well as the objective
rule, yet about half of never-delegators still endorsed a distrust-worded item. We
think this reflects a conflation built into the closed-item wording itself rather than
a contradiction in the evidence: the item (“I don’t fully trust the computer”) does
not distinguish distrust of the algorithm’s accuracy from a more diffuse distrust
of ceding the choice to any algorithmic system, including one the respondent oth-
erwise believes to be competent. Our quantitative expectation measures speak
specifically to the former and speak against it as the dominant driver; the qualita-
tive reason item plausibly picks up a mixture in which the latter, authority-related
sense of “distrust” predominates among never-delegators. Future work using
closed items that separate these two senses of distrust explicitly would allow
a cleaner test of this reading. Overall, the reasons of never-delegators fit the
treatment-level non-delegation pattern and are consistent with a subgroup that
weights agency concerns more heavily than expected performance. Among the
remaining items, finding the task too tedious appears (at a low level) only among
delegators (10.9% vs. 0%), consistent with an effort-saving channel, and open-text
responses repeatedly mention time pressure, a general choice-friction channel
that does not isolate a treatment-specific mechanism.
    The responsibility item needs a note, because it was worded according to
the hypothetical choice: participants who chose to decide themselves could
indicate that they wanted to be responsible for choosing, and participants who
chose to delegate could indicate that they did not want to be. The hypothetical
choice closely mirrors actual behavior (91% of never-delegators chose to decide
themselves, and 95% of always-delegators chose to delegate; Spearman correlation
between the hypothetical choice and the Part-2 delegation share: 0.68), so the
contrast between never-delegators and the rest largely contrasts reasons for
deciding oneself with reasons for delegating. Across all participants, 66.4% of
those who chose to decide themselves endorsed wanting to be responsible for the
choice, whereas 16.2% of those who chose to delegate endorsed not wanting to be.
We therefore read the item as the dominant stated reason for deciding oneself, in
a hypothetical choice that tracks the participant’s actual delegation behavior.

5     Discussion
5.1         Core finding
This preregistered study shows that preference-based personalization can deter
delegation rather than increase it. We observe this deterrence in two forms:
lower delegation at first contact and a larger subgroup of participants who never


                                                                                                      29
delegate under personalization.
    The first-contact finding is important because it captures behavior before
participants can learn from outcome feedback. At that stage, delegation is driven
primarily by how participants interpret the algorithmic rule itself. Our evidence
is consistent with the interpretation that an impersonal EV rule is initially easier
to accept as a legitimate external computation device than a profile-based person-
alized rule based on the user’s own revealed preferences. Even though subsequent
rounds show convergence in average delegation (driven by reduced delegation to
the EV algorithm), first-contact differences remain practically important because
initial adoption decisions can have path-dependent downstream effects in real-
world deployment where customers may not constantly be prompted to make
a delegation choice like in our experiment (think of immediate opt-out, lower
willingness to return, and weaker opportunity for learning-by-use).

5.2    Theoretical implications
Our findings advance the theoretical understanding of algorithm adoption in
three ways. First, they show that algorithm aversion is not a unitary phenomenon.
The literature has largely treated reluctance to rely on algorithms as a problem of
miscalibrated trust in algorithmic competence, to be remedied by demonstrating
accuracy, providing explanations, or allowing output adjustment (Dietvorst et al.,
2018; Leichtmann et al., 2023; Yeomans et al., 2019). Our evidence is most consis-
tent with a second, conceptually distinct channel: authority-based resistance. Par-
ticipants credited the personalized algorithm with superior preference-matching,
and never-delegators were, if anything, more satisfied with the preference input
that fed the algorithm—yet delegation was lower at first contact and categorical
opt-out was more than four times as prevalent. Interventions targeting accuracy
beliefs would not be expected to move this margin; indeed, among participants
with the strongest confidence in the algorithm’s competence, the treatment effect
was stronger, not weaker. Competence-oriented and authority-oriented barriers
therefore call for different remedies, and conflating them risks systematically
misdiagnosing adoption failures.
    Second, the results identify rule construal as a theoretically meaningful de-
terminant of delegation, distinct from the interface, control, and communication
features studied so far (Dietvorst et al., 2018; Fink et al., 2024; Hildebrand &
Bergner, 2021). Holding the delegation architecture fixed, merely changing what
the algorithm is said to optimize (an impersonal criterion versus the user’s own
preferences) changed behavior. In our perspective, this is because the decision
rule shapes whether delegation is construed as outsourcing a computation or
as surrendering one’s judgment, and only the latter construal affects the intrin-
sic value people attach to holding decision rights (Bartling et al., 2014; Frey et

                                        30
al., 2004; Owens et al., 2014). This links the algorithm-adoption literature to a
well-established body of evidence on choice authority and autonomy (Brehm,
1966; Deci & Ryan, 2000; Leotti et al., 2010) that has so far played little role in
explaining human–algorithm interaction, and it generates testable predictions:
any design cue that strengthens the judgment-substitution construal (e.g., empha-
sizing that the system “knows you” or “chooses as you would”) should increase
resistance among high-authority-valuation users, while cues that support the
computation-outsourcing construal should reduce it (testing these predictions
requires a design that manipulates such cues, which ours does not).
     Third, the heterogeneity structure of the effect, namely the concentration in
categorical opt-out rather than a uniform shift of delegation propensities, matters
for how personalization effects should be theorized and measured. If the intrin-
sic valuation of decision rights is heterogeneous (Bartling et al., 2014; Owens
et al., 2014), average treatment effects on delegation understate the behavioral
consequences of personalization for the affected subgroup, who exit entirely.
This has a general methodological implication for research on technology ac-
ceptance: distributional and subgroup analyses (such as our cumulative density
functions and never-delegator comparisons) can reveal adoption barriers that
mean comparisons conceal.
     Finally, our two-stage pattern suggests a simple integrative account of when
rule characteristics matter: construal of the decision rule dominates at adoption,
when no experiential evidence exists, whereas outcome feedback and the well-
documented asymmetric forgiveness of algorithmic errors (Dietvorst et al., 2015;
Renier et al., 2021) appear to dominate during continued use: in our exploratory
feedback analyses, participants withdrew from delegation disproportionately after
a bad outcome in both conditions, but we do not find that differential feedback
explains the convergence. Personalization thus operates on the adoption margin,
while error tolerance operates on the retention margin; theories of algorithm
acceptance should model these margins separately.

5.3    Interpretation and relation to prior work
The categorical non-delegation pattern is particularly informative: many partici-
pants entirely avoid delegation under personalization, consistent with work on
autonomy, responsibility, and control as drivers of acceptance. Exploratory post-
task reasons (Section 4.6) align with this interpretation, especially the stronger
emphasis among never-delegators on distrust and the prominence of respon-
sibility for the choice as a reason for deciding oneself, while concerns about
being algorithmically analyzed appear comparatively rare. Because these reason
measures are ex-post and refer to hypothetical choices, this evidence remains
suggestive rather than causal. Crucially, the lower delegation under personaliza-

                                        31
tion does not stem from participants doubting that the personalized algorithm
captured their preferences: they expected it to make significantly fewer non-
preferred choices than the EV algorithm (3.78 vs. 5.00 out of 10; p < 0.001),
while expectations about “completely weird” choices did not differ across condi-
tions. Participants thus credited the preference-based algorithm with successfully
matching their preferences, yet were no more willing—and at first contact less
willing—to delegate to it. This strengthens the interpretation that the relevant
barrier is the act of ceding choice authority itself rather than skepticism about
the algorithm’s accuracy. A complementary channel operates through how al-
gorithmic errors are treated once they occur: in both conditions, participants
withdrew from delegation disproportionately after the algorithm produced a bad
outcome (an exploratory finding; Section 4), echoing evidence that algorithmic
mistakes are forgiven less readily than one’s own (Dietvorst et al., 2018; Renier et
al., 2021). The preregistered prediction that participants would be less forgiving
of the preference-based than of the EV algorithm was not supported.
     Our findings are consistent with an earlier study on the same question (re-
ported as a chapter in Suraci’s PhD thesis), which motivated and informed the
design of the present preregistered experiment. In that study, the delegation gap
between EV and preference-based treatments was larger (about 20 percentage
points) and persisted over decisions. Recomputing the categorical non-delegation
contrast for that earlier sample also yields a significant difference (32/190 in
preference-based treatments vs. 17/187 in EV treatments; one-sided Boschloo
exact test, p = 0.015).9
     Two further accounts could produce similar behavior and deserve mention. A
responsibility-offloading account holds that people delegate to an objective rule
because it gives them someone to blame for bad outcomes, whereas a personalized
rule leaves them feeling responsible for its errors. Our data speak to this only
partly. Among participants who chose to decide themselves in the hypothetical
choice, which closely mirrors actual delegation behavior, 66.4% said they wanted
to be responsible for choosing, whereas only 16.2% of those who chose to delegate
said they did not want to be. Wanting to keep responsibility is thus the dominant
stated reason for deciding oneself, while a minority of delegators cites avoiding
responsibility, so some responsibility shifting among delegators is not excluded.
An HR experiment, moreover, found that blame avoidance was a weak motive for
delegation relative to algorithm aversion (Maasland & Weißmüller, 2022). Closer
to our setting, participants of Gogoll and Uhl (2018) had to delegate a task affecting
a third party and chose a machine over a human only about a quarter of the time
(26.5%). Neither lower perceived utility, lower trust, nor a weaker ability to pass on
   9
     Note that categorical non-delegation was not analyzed or reported in the earlier study, which

responsibility to a machine accounted for this. Because everyone had to delegate
to someone in that design, it speaks to the choice between agents rather than
to the choice between delegating and deciding oneself, but it likewise suggests
that a pure blame-shifting motive does not contribute too much to explaining
reluctance to rely on algorithms. We did not measure anticipated blame directly,
so offloading motives among delegators cannot be excluded.
    A psychological-ownership account is also compatible with ours: if a system
decides as the user would, the resulting choice may feel less like the user’s
own. Work on AI-generated text finds that users do not feel they own what the
AI produced and that their sense of ownership rises with their own influence
on the output (Draxler et al., 2024). This is conceptually close to our choice-
authority account. That evidence concerns text generation rather than delegated
choices, though, and personalization did not alter ownership there, so it does
not directly predict our result. Ownership and anticipated-blame measures are
natural additions for future work.

5.4    Limitations
The findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the study
is an online experiment with stylized financial lotteries, so external validity
to field settings should be interpreted cautiously. The Part-2 lottery choices
were real and incentivized, not hypothetical vignettes: one round was paid out
for real money, contingent on the participant’s own choice or, if delegated, on
the algorithm’s choice, so the delegation decisions we analyze carried genuine
financial consequences rather than being merely described. What remains stylized
is the task domain: our lotteries abstract away features of real investment or
medical decisions such as ambiguous or delayed payoffs, product complexity,
and social or institutional framing (e.g., a named bank or physician), any of
which could independently affect how the loss of choice authority is experienced.
This abstraction is what let us isolate the construal mechanism cleanly (see the
theoretical framework above), but it also means we cannot yet speak to whether
the same first-contact resistance and categorical opt-out would emerge, and at
what magnitude, once real-world complexity and institutional trust cues are
reintroduced; we return to this as a direction for future work below.
     Second, although behavioral outcomes are well suited for studying reliance,
the design does not causally identify the psychological mechanism behind first-
contact hesitation or categorical non-delegation (e.g., perceived objectivity, pro-
filing concerns, or accountability salience); our expectation data speak against
accuracy-based accounts, and the post-task reasons point toward choice authority,
but a direct manipulation of authority salience remains for future work. Relatedly,
the two algorithm descriptions differ not only in decision rule but also in linguistic

                                         33
register: the EV description is phrased mechanically (“works out how much each
option would yield on average”), while the preference-based description is phrased
more personally (“analyses your choices…and selects the best option for you”).
This asymmetry is partly deliberate. In our earlier study, the preference-based
description said only that the algorithm would use “modern decision science”
and the participant’s choices “to model your behaviour, and selects the lottery
that it predicts you would select”, whereas the EV description said that it “uses
mathematics to work out how much each lottery would yield on average, and
selects the lottery for which this is highest”. That earlier contrast left open that
the advantage of the EV algorithm reflected the missing optimization claim in
the preference-based description rather than its personalization. We therefore
added “selects the best option for you” to the preference-based description in the
present study, so that both algorithms are described as optimizing, one for an
impersonal criterion and one for the participant. The first-contact gap remained
alive (11.1 percentage points, against about 20 in the earlier study), so the missing
optimization claim cannot fully account for it, and the two studies also differ
in other respects, which prevents us from clearly attributing the smaller gap to
the changed wording. The two descriptions still differ in register, and we cannot
fully rule out that some of the effect reflects this difference in tone rather than
construal of the rule per se. A cleaner test would equate the two descriptions’
register explicitly in future work.
    Third, the EV and preference-based treatments differ along several dimensions
at once: personalization narrowly defined, the perceived objectivity of the decision
criterion, whether the rule explicitly draws on the user’s own prior choices
(profiling), the criterion being optimized, and possibly expectations about financial
performance. Our design therefore identifies a difference between two decision
rules rather than a pure personalization effect, and it cannot tell whether the
objective rule raises or the personalized rule lowers delegation relative to a
neutral baseline. The data help to narrow down the candidates, but they do
not eliminate them. Participants in the preference-based condition expected the
algorithm to match their preferences better, not worse, which speaks against
a lower-competence account at the level of preference matching. We did not,
however, elicit expectations about payoffs, and the EV description promises the
highest average yield, so a payoff-expectation account cannot be fully excluded.
    A profiling-aversion account is unlikely to be the dominant driver, because
the profiling-related reason item (“I don’t like to be analysed by an algorithm”)
was selected only infrequently. What remains is the combination of perceived
objectivity and the preference-based rule’s explicit reference to the user’s own
judgment, which is the dimension our choice-authority account targets. The
present design cannot separate the two, and future work could manipulate them
independently (e.g., an objective rule that is explicitly based on the user’s own

                                         34
revealed data). Relatedly, outcome feedback is generated by the rule a participant
is assigned to, so it can differ across conditions. In our data the difference in
exposure to zero payoffs is small (40.3% vs. 36.2% of delegated decisions), but we
cannot exclude that feedback contributes to the convergence in average delegation
that can be seen in Figure 2. The feedback caveat does not extend to our two central
results: the first-contact difference is measured before any outcome feedback
exists, and categorical non-delegation by definition involves participants who
never received algorithm-generated feedback.
    Fourth, low choice quality in the self-chosen decisions (roughly two-thirds
of self-made choices were dominated, see Section 4.2) raises the question of
whether some non-delegation reflects task difficulty rather than a genuine val-
uation of choice authority. We consider this unlikely to explain categorical
non-delegation specifically: never-delegators’ non-dominated choice rate was
statistically indistinguishable from that of participants who delegated at least
once (participant-level averages of 34.7% for the 22 never-delegators vs. 33.6%
for the 170 participants who delegated at least once but also made at least one
decision themselves; Welch t = −0.25, p = 0.806; Wilcoxon p = 0.669), so never-
delegators were not making better- or worse-informed choices than delegators,
and if anything, participants who found the task difficult would be expected to
delegate more, not less, which is the opposite of what categorical non-delegation
implies. We cannot rule out that task difficulty contributes to delegation decisions
among participants who delegate only sometimes, which future work could probe
directly.
    Fifth, the present analyses focus on short-run repeated interaction (eight
incentivized rounds within a single session); longer-run adaptation to personalized
systems remains an open question. Our within-session data already show that the
treatment gap in average delegation narrows after the first round, which is at least
consistent with some short-run adaptation as participants gain outcome feedback.
However, this convergence occurred over a handful of rounds in one sitting and
should not be extrapolated to long-term human–computer interaction in real
investment scenarios, where the relevant time scale is months or years rather
than minutes. Extended real-world use plausibly triggers mechanisms absent
from our design in both directions: on one hand, an accumulating track record of
satisfactory outcomes and growing familiarity with a personalized advisor could
erode authority-based resistance and allow accuracy-based trust to dominate,
consistent with evidence that reliance builds gradually through experience; on
the other hand, real investment relationships involve continued or deepening
profiling (the system knowing progressively more about the user), higher stakes,
and switching costs that could instead sustain or amplify the loss of choice
authority we document. Because our design cannot adjudicate between these
possibilities, we see longitudinal field studies that track delegation to personalized

                                         35
versus objective financial decision aids over repeated, consequential interactions
as a necessary next step, and we flag this explicitly as a direction for future research
below rather than assume our first-contact and short-run results generalize to
long-term use.
    Sixth, our preregistration set the sample-size target (120 participants per
condition, no fewer than 100 after exclusions) without reporting the underlying
power analysis. The power analysis based on the effect sizes of our earlier study
(Appendix C) made the design look adequately powered ex ante, but it also shows
that the first-contact gap we observed (11.1 percentage points) lies below its
minimum detectable effect (at 80% power) of 16–17 percentage points, which
is a natural explanation for why the confirmed effect is significant only in the
preregistered one-sided versions of the tests. Seventh, our sample was recruited
through Prolific from a UK-based participant pool; while we see no principled
reason to expect the direction of the effect to be culture- or platform-specific,
the magnitude of resistance to ceding choice authority could plausibly vary with
cultural background, familiarity with algorithmic systems, or the demographic
composition of online panels, and we have not tested this.

5.5    Implications and future research
For designing algorithmic decision systems, the results imply that personalization
should not be treated as a uniformly beneficial acceptance lever. In settings where
objective criteria are salient and normatively legitimate, objective optimization
may face lower initial resistance than preference-based personalization. This has
direct implications for the design and communication of decision aids in finance
and other high-stakes domains.
    These extensions are speculative given the abstract, single-domain, and time-
constrained nature of our lottery task, and we offer them as hypotheses for
domain-specific replication rather than as established implications. Financial robo-
advisors routinely face the choice between objective portfolio rules (e.g., mean-
variance optimization) and preference-based personalization derived from risk
profiling questionnaires (Capponi et al., 2022; Rühr, 2020). Our findings suggest
that the very feature intended to increase subjective fit—tailoring the algorithm to
the user’s profile—may inadvertently encroach on users’ sense of choice authority,
deterring precisely those users who value exercising active control over their
decisions. The same tension arises whenever algorithmic decision aids must be
voluntarily adopted: in healthcare, treatment recommender systems can follow
clinical-evidence guidelines or patient-preference protocols; in hiring, screening
tools can apply standardized criteria or adaptively weight candidate profiles. In
each case, the assumption that preference alignment promotes adoption deserves
empirical scrutiny rather than being taken as a design axiom.

                                          36
     Future research should test mechanism-targeted interventions at first con-
tact, including reframing of personalization logic, responsibility framing, and
alternative explanation formats. The construal account developed here suggests
concrete candidates: presenting a personalized system as a tool that implements
the user’s prior decisions (computation-outsourcing framing) rather than one
that decides as the user would (judgment-substitution framing) should reduce
authority-based resistance without changing the underlying rule. Testing this
requires a design that varies the framing while holding the rule fixed, so it is a
task for future work and not something the present data can provide. Measuring
individuals’ intrinsic valuation of decision rights (Bartling et al., 2014; Owens et
al., 2014) before exposure would additionally allow a direct test of the predicted
heterogeneity. It should also examine whether the non-delegator subgroup is
stable across domains, stakes, and cultural contexts.

C     Appendix: Ex-ante power analysis
Our preregistration set the sample-size target (120 participants per condition, no
fewer than 100 after exclusions) without stating an explicit power analysis. We
provide that analysis here: In our earlier study reported in Suraci’s PhD thesis,
the EV-versus-preference-based gap was approximately 22 percentage points
at first contact (67.4% vs. 45.8%, pooling across an additional feedback-timing
factor absent from the present design) and approximately 19 percentage points in
average delegation across all decisions.
    At n = 100–120 per arm, the minimum detectable risk difference at 80%
power (one-sided α = 0.05) for a first-contact-type proportion comparison is
approximately 16–17 percentage points, comfortably below the earlier study’s
first-contact gap; correspondingly, power to re-detect an effect of the earlier
study’s magnitude was approximately 91–95% (exact-test simulation; 93–96%
under the standard normal approximation). For the average-delegation (Wilcoxon–
Mann–Whitney-type) comparison, the analogous minimum detectable effect is
approximately 10–11 percentage points (using the earlier study’s within-condition
variance), again well below the earlier study’s roughly 19-percentage-point gap,


                                             46
with approximately 99.7–99.9% power to re-detect an effect of that size. On this
basis, a sample of 100–120 per condition looked more than adequately powered
at the design stage.
     With hindsight, the first-contact gap we actually observed in the present
design was smaller than the earlier study’s gap (11.1 percentage points, 66.9% vs.
55.8%, p = 0.041 one-sided), falling below the design’s 16–17-percentage-point de-
tection threshold, which offers a natural explanation for why this confirmed effect
is significant only under the preregistered one-sided tests. We deliberately stop
short of computing a “power” figure for this realized effect itself: retrospective
power calculated from a study’s own observed effect size is a deterministic func-
tion of the p-value and provides no additional information beyond it (Hoenig &
Heisey, 2001). For the same reason, we do not interpret the null average-delegation
